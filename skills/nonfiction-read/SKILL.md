---
name: nonfiction-read
description: Read a nonfiction book or book proposal in Tabularia — memoir, history, self-help, business, science, how-to — for whether it keeps its promise to the reader, makes its case, and supports its claims. Use when the Writer of a nonfiction book asks whether it works, whether the argument holds, whether a chapter earns its place, what is missing, or asks for a developmental read of nonfiction.
---

<what-this-is>

A developmental read for a book that argues, teaches or tells true events. A
novel is read against its shape; a nonfiction book is read against its
promise: what the reader was told they would understand, be able to do, or
come away believing. Every chapter either moves the reader toward that or it
does not.

You never rewrite here. Findings are comments the Writer reads in Studio.

</what-this-is>

<find-the-promise>

1. `tabularia_project_metadata` — the Books, their descriptions, and the
   Chapters. A Book description is often the promise in the Writer's own words.
2. `tabularia_list_comments` and `tabularia_list_anchors` — a Project started
   from a nonfiction outline, such as the Book Proposal, carries a note on each
   section saying what it is for. Read against those, as `structural-read` does
   for a novel.
3. Read the introduction or first chapter with `tabularia_read_window`. The
   promise is usually stated there; if it is not stated anywhere, that is the
   first finding.

Say the promise back to the Writer in one sentence before going further, and
let them correct it. Everything after depends on it.

</find-the-promise>

<what-editors-find-most>

- **No clear promise, or two.** The introduction offers one book and the
  chapters deliver another, or two books are sharing the pages.
- **A chapter that does not advance it.** Interesting, well written, and
  answering a question the reader was not asking. Name what the chapter
  contributes in a line; if you cannot, that is the finding.
- **Claims without support.** A statistic, a study, a quotation or a
  historical fact stated with no source. List them with their Blocks. Do not
  say whether a claim is true unless the Writer asks you to check it, and then
  say where you checked.
- **The same point made in several chapters.** Search for its key phrase with
  `tabularia_search` to find every place it is made.
- **Too much the Writer, too little the reader.** In memoir this is the
  difference between what happened and what it meant; in advice books, between
  the author's story and what the reader should do with it.
- **An ending that stops rather than lands.** The last chapter should pay off
  the promise, not add one more topic.

Three to six findings is a useful read. Anchor each with `tabularia_add_comment`
on the passage it is about, with the Blocks it rests on.

</what-editors-find-most>

<for-a-proposal>

A book proposal is read by an acquiring editor, not a reader. Check that it
answers what they ask: what the book is in a paragraph, who it is for and how
many of them there are, why this author, which books it sits beside, and a
chapter-by-chapter outline with a sample chapter. Name any of these that is
missing or thin. Market figures and credentials are the Writer's to supply;
never invent them.

</for-a-proposal>

<what-not-to-do>

- Do not apply fiction structure. There is no midpoint reversal in a cookbook.
- Do not fact-check silently. A claim you believe is wrong is a comment that
  says why, never a change.
- Do not propose edits. If the Writer wants a chapter reworked, that is a
  Draft; `line-edit` covers sentence work.

</what-not-to-do>
